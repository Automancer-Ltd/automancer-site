Say "automation project" to most small-business owners and they brace for
five figures and six months. Sometimes that's the right size. Quite often,
though, the job is one annoying workflow, and the honest price for one
annoying workflow is a lot smaller than the fear.

## How much does a small automation project cost?

A small, well-scoped automation project can cost under £1,000. We quoted a
leave-request workflow for
[a ~600-person supported-living care provider](/work/care-provider-transformation)
at 20 hours at £45 an hour: £900 in total.

That figure comes straight off the estimate, not from a pricing page
designed to look friendly. One caveat, because we'd rather you heard it
from us: the module is still in active build as part of a much larger
programme of work. So £900 is the quoted price, not a number we're
reporting back after go-live.

## What £900 buys: one workflow, start to finish

The job is to replace paper and spreadsheet leave tracking. Written down, it
sounds trivial: someone asks for time off, someone else says yes. In a care
provider with hundreds of support workers, it's forms going missing,
approvals sitting in whoever's inbox they landed in, a calendar nobody
fully trusts, and no record to point at when two people remember a
conversation differently.

The workflow does four things:

- **Request.** Staff ask for leave in one place, not on a scrap of paper.
- **Routed approval.** The request goes to the person who should approve
  it, not whoever happens to be nearest.
- **Calendar.** Approved leave lands on a calendar view the rota can be
  planned against.
- **Audit trail.** Every request, decision and notification is recorded,
  so "I asked in March" can be checked rather than argued about.

That's the whole thing. No new HR system, no rota planner, no payroll
integration bolted on because it seemed a shame not to.

## Small and cheap is a scoping decision, not a corner cut

The £900 isn't low because anything was skipped. It's low because the
scope was disciplined from the first conversation. There's no trick to
it: it's scope discipline, which is less exciting than magic and
considerably more reliable. Three things kept it small:

- **One workflow, not a platform.** Request, route, approve, record. Rota
  planning, payroll and HR records were left out on purpose.
- **Approval rules that could be written down.** Who approves leave for
  which staff was a known answer, not a research project. If it had been
  contested internally, settling it would have been the expensive part,
  and it would have had to happen before any build.
- **A defined place to live.** It's one workstream among roughly
  eighteen, not an attempt to be the system that fixes everything at once.

Some things genuinely can't be this small. Turning messy exports into
clean, deduplicated records, or building the core platform a whole
organisation runs on, costs more because there is more engineering in it.
The same provider's core platform is a far bigger piece of work than its
leave module. The point isn't that everything is cheap. It's that a small
problem deserves a small price, not one inflated because "automation"
sounds expensive.

## How to spot your own under-£1,000 candidate

Before you ask anyone to quote, put your candidate through four questions:

1. **Can you describe it as "when X happens, do Y"?** If the whole job fits
   in that sentence, it's small. If it needs its own users, permissions
   and data model, it isn't, and that's fine. It's just a different
   kind of project.
2. **Do you already know who decides what?** If the approval logic is
   clear, the build is mostly plumbing. If it's disputed, sort that out
   first, because no software settles an argument your team hasn't had
   yet.
3. **Can it live inside something you already have?** A workflow that
   plugs into the tools you already use stays small. One that needs a new
   system to sit in grows.
4. **What does the manual version actually cost you?** Count the hours and
   the mistakes honestly. If the answer is "not much", leave it alone,
   however satisfying it would be to fix.

If the first three answers are yes and the fourth is "more than we'd
like to admit", you probably have a small job on your hands, not a
programme.
