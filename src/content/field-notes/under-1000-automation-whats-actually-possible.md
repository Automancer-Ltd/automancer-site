## What a small job looks like: one workflow, start to finish

Take leave requests. Written down, it sounds trivial: someone asks for
time off, someone else says yes. In a business with dozens or hundreds of
staff, it's forms going missing, approvals sitting in whoever's inbox they
landed in, a calendar nobody fully trusts, and no record to point at when
two people remember a conversation differently.

A well-scoped version does four things:

- **Request.** Staff ask for leave in one place, not on a scrap of paper.
- **Routed approval.** The request goes to the person who should approve
  it, not whoever happens to be nearest.
- **Calendar.** Approved leave lands on a calendar view the rota can be
  planned against.
- **Audit trail.** Every request and decision is recorded, so "I asked in
  March" can be checked rather than argued about.

That's the whole thing. No new HR system, no payroll integration bolted on
because it seemed a shame not to. Approval routing is one of the jobs we
list as a good fit for a Sprint, alongside enquiry-to-quote handling and
the report that currently eats someone's Friday.

It's not a niche problem, either. Support-worker leave requests are one
of the pieces in active build for
[a ~600-person supported-living care provider](/work/care-provider-transformation),
as one piece of a programme that runs to roughly eighteen governed
workstreams. Small pieces sit perfectly happily inside big programmes.
They just have to be scoped as small pieces.

## Small and cheap is a scoping decision, not a corner cut

A small job isn't small because anything was skipped. It's small because
the scope was disciplined from the first conversation. There's no trick to
it: it's scope discipline, which is less exciting than magic and
considerably more reliable. Three things keep a job small:

- **One workflow, not a platform.** Request, route, approve, record.
  Everything adjacent is left out on purpose, and written down as left out.
- **Rules that can be written down.** If you can say who approves what,
  the build is mostly plumbing. If that's contested internally, settling
  it is the expensive part, and it has to happen before anyone builds.
- **A place it already lives.** A workflow that plugs into the tools you
  use every day stays small. One that needs a new system to sit in grows.

Some things genuinely can't be this small. The same care provider's core
platform turned messy CSVs into 294 clean carer records and auto-created
588 supervision schedules. That's a System / Agent Build, from £4,500,
because there is more engineering in it. The point isn't that everything is cheap.
It's that a small problem deserves a small price, not one inflated because
"automation" sounds expensive.

## How to spot your own small candidate

Before you ask anyone to quote, put your candidate through four questions:

1. **Can you describe it as "when X happens, do Y"?** If the whole job fits
   in that sentence, it's small. If it needs its own users, permissions
   and data model, it isn't, and that's fine. It's just a different
   kind of project.
2. **Do you already know who decides what?** If the approval logic is
   clear, the build is mostly plumbing. If it's disputed, sort that out
   first, because no software settles an argument your team hasn't had
   yet.
3. **Can it live inside something you already have?** If yes, it stays
   small. If it needs a new home, budget for building the home too.
4. **What does the manual version actually cost you?** Count the hours and
   the mistakes honestly. If the answer is "not much", leave it alone,
   however satisfying it would be to fix.

If the first three answers are yes and the fourth is "more than we'd
like to admit", you probably have a small job on your hands, not a
programme.
