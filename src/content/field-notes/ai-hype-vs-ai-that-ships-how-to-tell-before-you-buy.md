If you run a small business in the UK, your inbox is probably full of people offering to sprinkle AI over your operations. Most of them have a slick slide deck, a vague daily rate, and nothing actually running in production.

## How do you tell AI hype from AI that actually ships?

AI that ships can be shown to you running, monitored, and backed up in production today, whereas AI hype lives almost entirely in slide decks, prototypes, and vague promises about the future.

That is the clearest tell when you are fielding pitches. Listen to what a vendor wants to talk about. If they spend the meeting talking in grand, breathless terms about what AI might do for your industry, they are selling a story. If they want to talk about your messy spreadsheets, your handoffs, and what happens on a busy Monday morning, you are probably talking to an engineer.

There is a lot of theatre around AI right now. Vendors love a polished demo on clean sample data that looks enchanted for a few minutes and falls over the first time a real customer types a postcode in the wrong box. Building a prototype that impresses in a meeting is easy. Building software that is audited, monitored, backed up, and tested so it keeps working quietly overnight is the actual job.

At Automancer, we put the magic joke in the company name and puncture it immediately. It's not magic. It's very good engineering, pointed at the boring, expensive, error-prone work that eats your week.

## Three questions to ask before you buy

You do not need to understand how language models work to evaluate an AI vendor. That is their job. Your job is to ask plain operational questions and watch how comfortable they are answering them.

- **What is actually live, and what is still in a slide deck?** Ask the vendor to draw a clean line between work that is live in production, work that is in build, and ideas that only exist on slides. Honest engineers are picky about delivery states because the gap between a demo and a live system is where most of the work sits.
- **What happens when it breaks?** Every system hits an edge case eventually: a supplier sends a broken CSV, an integration changes, or a server needs a restart. Ask how the vendor will know it broke before your team does. Listen for boring, reassuring words: monitoring, backups, testing, and staged delivery so you see it working before it goes live.
- **Who is accountable when a decision is wrong?** Automation is brilliant for routine admin and terrible as a scapegoat. Ask where the boundary sits between what the software does on its own and what needs a person to approve it. If the vendor cannot draw that line cleanly, they are asking you to hand over control without a safety net.

## What I would do before signing anything

If I were running a small business and fielding AI pitches right now, I would ignore the hype, skip the year-long programmes, and insist on starting small.

Pick one boring, expensive bottleneck your team hates doing by hand. Ask the vendor to scope it in plain English, state the price upfront, explain how they monitor and back up live systems, and show you where a human stays in control. If they cannot answer without retreating into buzzwords, keep your wallet closed.
